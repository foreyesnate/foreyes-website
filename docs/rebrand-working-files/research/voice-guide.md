# ForEyes Books & Billing: Brand Voice Guide

For copywriters building the new site. Every rule here is derived from how ForEyes Solutions writes today (crawl of foreyessolutions.com and start.foreyessolutions.com/invision, 39 page files). Text in "quotation marks" attributed to a page is verbatim from the current site, including its punctuation. Anything labelled **Draft** is new suggested copy, not a quote.

Pages read in full: Home, About, Bookkeeping, Billing Services, Startups, FAQ, the INVISION landing page, and two blog posts (P&L red flags, growing A/R). Counts marked "sitewide" were taken across all 39 crawled files; counts marked "nine pages" cover only the pages read in full.

## 1. Who is speaking, and to whom

### The speaker

A small, remote, eyecare-only back-office firm founded by an optometrist, speaking as "we". The founder, Wade Weisz, OD, is the source of the firm's authority and appears by name. Clients talk about "Wade and his team", so the voice is a team with a named OD at the head of it, not a faceless firm.

How the site describes itself:

- "Founded by an OD who has spent 25+ years running successful practices, we don’t just talk the talk–we’ve walked the walk." (Home)
- "Built by an OD, for ECPs." (INVISION)
- "We’re not just number crunchers–we’re industry insiders, strategic thinkers, and trusted advisors who know the ins and outs of running a successful eyecare practice." (About)
- "I’ve lived your challenges and built this company to solve them, with accuracy you can trust and support that feels like part of your team." (Wade's signed quote, About)

Stance to hold on the new site: **a fellow practice owner who now runs your books and your billing.** Peer first, vendor second. The speaker knows what an exam lane, an optical, a vision plan and a front desk are, and does not need them explained.

### The reader

An independent optometrist who owns the practice, or the practice manager who runs its back office. The site addresses three situations: a first-time owner or startup, an established single-location owner, and a multi-location group.

What the site assumes about this reader:

- They are clinically expert and financially under-served. "You became an optometrist to care for patients–not to spend late nights reconciling receipts and second-guessing your financials." (Home)
- They do not trust their numbers. "Too many practices operate with incomplete books, misclassified expenses, and reports that don’t tell the whole story." (About)
- They look at reports but do not use them. "for many optometrists and practice owners, the P&L becomes something you glance at, not something you use." (Blog)
- They are short on time and staff. "If your staff is stretched too thin or your denial rate feels too high, it’s time to put your billing needs in the hands of our specialists." (Billing)
- They want to stay in control. "Our services are designed to free up your time while keeping you confidently in the driver’s seat." (FAQ)

Write to one owner, as "you". Treat them as an intelligent adult running a real business who has been handed generic reports for years. Never write to "businesses", "customers" or "users".

### Person and point of view

| Who | How they are referred to | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The firm | "we", "our", "us" | "We manage your daily transactions" |
| The reader | "you", "your practice", "your team" | "Your job is to review reports and ask questions." |
| The founder | Third person by full name with credential, then "Wade" | "Founder Wade Weisz, OD, has over 25 years of experience" |
| The founder, speaking | "I", only in a signed, quoted statement | "I’ve been there" |
| The reader, asking | First person in FAQ questions | "My claim denial rate seems high. How can you help?" |
| The reader's customers | "patients" (never "customers" or "clients") | "focus on what matters most–your patients" |
| ForEyes' customers | "clients", "practices" | "trusted by practices across the country" |

## 2. Voice attributes

The current site contains two registers. The older one (Home, About, Billing, Startups, FAQ) is warm but padded: en-dash asides, "we don't just X, we Y", strings of benefit adjectives. The newer one (the Bookkeeping page, the "Remote, Nationwide Assistance" block, the A/R blog post) is concrete, short-sentenced and mechanical in the good sense: it shows how the work is done. **The new site should be written in the newer register and keep the warmth of the older one.** The six attributes below describe that blend.

### 2.1 Owner to owner

**What it means.** We have run practices. We describe the reader's situation from inside it, using the details only an owner would know, and we never talk down or sell from outside.

**On the site.**
> "I’ve been there, and know how that uncertainty impacts not just your bottom line but your ability to lead, grow, and sleep at night." (About, Wade Weisz, OD)

> "It comes from Wade Weisz, OD, and his 25-plus years in the owner's seat." (Bookkeeping)

**Too far.** Chummy insider talk, or using the OD credential as the answer to everything.
> Draft counter-example: "Hey doc, we get it. Insurance is the WORST, right? Lucky for you, our founder is an OD, so we just *know*."

Rule of thumb: state the founder credential once per page, then prove the experience through specifics.

### 2.2 Plain and clear

**What it means.** One of the firm's five stated values is "Clarity Over Complexity": "We believe financial guidance should be clear, understandable, and empowering. We remove the noise and bring simplicity to the complex". Say what a thing is and what it does in everyday words. Define an accounting or billing term the first time it appears.

**On the site.**
> "Your profit and loss statement shows how your practice is performing month to month. Your balance sheet shows what you own and what you owe." (Blog, P&L red flags)

> "Good books explain your practice instead of merely recording it." (Bookkeeping)

**Too far, in one direction:** talking down.
> Draft counter-example: "Numbers can be scary! Don't worry, we'll handle all the complicated math for you."

**Too far, in the other:** abstract consultancy language. The current site does this:
> "When combined with our accounting solutions, you get a holistic view of your practice’s financial health" (Billing)

### 2.3 Specific and operational

**What it means.** Show the mechanism: which dollars, which report, which day of the month, who reviews it. Specifics are what separate ForEyes from a generalist and they are the strongest copy on the current site.

**On the site.**
> "Exams, optical sales, and contact lenses all deposit into the same bank account, which makes it hard to see which part of your practice carries its weight." (Bookkeeping)

> "Before any P&L or balance sheet reaches your client portal, a dedicated quality control manager reviews it." (Bookkeeping)

> "Once a claim is denied — even if your resubmission is perfect — the clock resets to 180 days." (Blog, A/R)

**Too far.** A jargon dump that turns the page into an accounting manual.
> Draft counter-example: "We perform accrual-basis GL reconciliation, post adjusting journal entries, and remit ERA/EOB data against CPT-level charge lines per payer contract."

Rule of thumb: one concrete detail per paragraph, in words the owner uses at the front desk.

### 2.4 Direct and quietly confident

**What it means.** Short declarative claims, stated once, without hedging and without superlatives. Confidence comes from being specific about what we do and plain about what we do not do.

**On the site.**
> "We don’t “also work with eyecare professionals.” We only work with them, and that industry expertise shows." (Home, About)

> "This one is non-negotiable: do not work for free." (Blog, A/R)

> "Yes. We don’t just hand you a report. Our team works alongside you to implement changes and ensure improvements stick." (FAQ)

**Too far.** Swagger, superlatives, or knocking competitors. The current site crosses this line:
> "far faster than many other Nashville, TN bookkeepers can deliver" (Bookkeeping)

> "To become the leading accounting, advisory, and practice solutions partner for the eyecare industry" (About)

### 2.5 On the owner's side

**What it means.** The promise is relief plus control: we take the work, you keep the decisions. Problems are framed as fixable process gaps, never as the owner's failure, and never as a scare.

**On the site.**
> "Your job is to review reports and ask questions. Your account manager handles the rest and will meet whenever a number needs explaining." (Bookkeeping)

> "Every one of these has a root cause inside your practice. That's actually good news — because what happens internally can be changed internally." (Blog, A/R)

> "The goal isn’t to cut staff—it’s to make sure your team structure supports your patient flow." (Blog, P&L)

**Too far, in one direction:** fear selling. The INVISION page does this:
> "Financial records are scattered across multiple systems, making tax season a nightmare." (INVISION)

**Too far, in the other:** gushing. Within one FAQ page, answers open with "Yes!", "Of course.", "Absolutely.", "Of course,", "Absolutely.", "Absolutely." and "Of course!". Answer the question in the first sentence instead.

### 2.6 A light touch of wit

**What it means.** An occasional dry turn of phrase or a single vision pun, usually in a headline. It is seasoning. Most paragraphs have none.

**On the site.**
> "Keep Your A/R Under Control Before It Keeps You" (Blog H2)

> "Payments and paychecks do not wait on geography." (Remote, Nationwide Assistance block)

> "The practices with the lowest denial rates treat each one as an operational memo from the payer." (Blog, A/R)

**Too far.** Stacking puns, or punning where the reader needs a fact. See section 3.
> Draft counter-example: "See your way clear to 20/20 books with a visionary team that keeps an eye on every lens of your practice."

### 3.2 How often it is actually used

- It lives in **headlines, taglines and closing bands**, almost never in explanatory body copy. Across the nine pages read in full, deliberate vision wordplay appears about a dozen times in total, and nearly all of it sits on Home and About.
- The two flagship service pages are close to pun-free. Bookkeeping has one ("A Second Set of Eyes"). Billing Services has none outside the shared footer band. Both blog posts have none. The best-written pages on the site are the ones with the least wordplay.
- Repetition comes from templating, not from writers reaching for puns: "See Further. Move Faster. Grow Smarter." alone accounts for 35 appearances.
- "clear picture" and "clarity" are over-used to the point of becoming filler (see section 7).

### 3.3 Rules for the new site

1. **One deliberate vision pun per page, at most.** The brand name already counts as the standing pun. A page with none is fine and is the norm for service detail, pricing, FAQ and blog body.
2. **Headlines and sign-offs only.** Never inside an explanation of how something works, a price, a timeline, a step in a process, or anything to do with dropping tax.
3. **It must be literally true as well as punny.** "A Second Set of Eyes" works because a quality control manager really does review every report. "Receivables Read Through an Eyecare Lens" works because the reviewers know eyecare. "Our (20/20) Vision" is only a joke, so retire it.
4. **Never stack.** One sight image per sentence and per screen. "You keep your focus on the chair. We keep the financial strategy in focus." is the limit; it only works because the two halves mean different things.
5. **Keep one master line.** "Bring your financials into focus" is the strongest, is already the hero, and extends naturally ("Bring Your Receivables Into Focus"). Use the family once per page at most. For the rebrand, both flagships fit it: books into focus, receivables into focus.
6. **Cut "See Further. Move Faster. Grow Smarter." from the global footer band.** If kept, it appears once on the site. It is the single most repeated line today and the weakest of the taglines because it says nothing about books or billing.
7. **No clinical or patient-facing eye jokes.** Nothing about prescriptions, blurry vision, eye charts, glasses, "four eyes", or blindness. The reader treats sight loss for a living. Retire "flying blind" and "in the dark".
8. **Ration the stock words.** "clarity" no more than once per page. "clear picture" no more than once per page. Prefer the thing itself: say what the owner will be able to see ("which location is carrying the others", "what you are owed and how old it is").
9. **Never pun in a CTA.** CTA labels say what happens next. "Claim Control" fails this test.

## 4. Vocabulary and naming rules

### 4.1 Company name

| Form | Sitewide count | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| "ForEyes Solutions" | 233 in 38 files | Current standard in body copy, footers, testimonials |
| "For Eyes Solutions" | 47 in 16 files | Almost entirely in page titles and meta descriptions; one blog H2 ("For Eyes Solutions is Dedicated to Helping Eyecare Professionals Succeed") |
| "For Eyes" alone | In one client testimonial (Cristine Areola, OD: "For Eyes has been a great resource") | Client's own words |
| "ForEyes" alone | Used as short form: "Why ForEyes", "ForEyes Financial Consulting", "Since joining ForEyes" | Accepted short form |
| "foreyes" lowercase | "About foreyes" label on INVISION page, plus URLs and handles | Error outside URLs |

Rules for the new brand:

- Full name: **ForEyes Books & Billing**. One word, capital F and capital E, no space. Always the ampersand, never "and". "Books" before "Billing", always.
- Short form after first mention: **ForEyes**, or simply "we". Never "For Eyes", "Foreyes", "FOREYES", "4Eyes", or initials.
- Never "ForEyes Solutions" on the new site, except where a testimonial or a dated press mention uses it (see below).
- Page titles and meta descriptions must use the same spelling as the body. The current split (body "ForEyes", metadata "For Eyes") must not be carried over.
- Testimonials are quoted as given. Where a client wrote "For Eyes" or "ForEyes Solutions", get the client's approval before changing it rather than silently editing.
- Possessive: "ForEyes'" is awkward; write "our" instead.

### 4.2 People and credentials

- **"Wade Weisz, OD"** on first mention (comma, no periods: OD, never O.D.), then "Wade". Used this way throughout: "Founder Wade Weisz, OD, has over 25 years of experience".
- Client names take the same form: "Ryan Corte, OD", "Nona Kalfayan, OD", "Brandon Luu, OD", "Cristine Areola, OD". The INVISION page breaks the pattern with "Dr. Athena Brasfield / President & CEO, Coachella Valley Optometry". Standardise on "Name, OD", adding title and practice on a second line where available. Do not combine "Dr." and "OD".
- **OD / ODs** as a noun for the reader is established and friendly: "Founded by an OD", "Smart Reads for ODs", "straight from the ODs themselves". Article is "an OD".
- **"ECPs"** appears only on the INVISION page (2 uses: "Built by an OD, for ECPs.", "Why ECPs Trust ForEyes Solutions"). Avoid on the main site; it is trade-magazine shorthand.

| Term | Usage on site | Rule |

| **eyecare practice / eyecare professionals** | The dominant general term | Use when ophthalmology and optical are meant too |

### 4.5 Named process steps and roles

The site capitalises a small set of terms as if they were product features. Keep that, and keep it consistent.

- **Discovery Call**: capitalised 92 times, lowercase 13 times, and "Book a discovery Call" 3 times (a casing error on audience pages). Rule: capital D, capital C, everywhere. Described as "a complimentary discovery call" (FAQ) and "Free 45-minute consultation" (INVISION).
- **Custom Chart of Accounts**: capitalised 9 times as ForEyes' own structure ("codes each one to your Custom Chart of Accounts"), lowercase 8 times as the general concept ("What is a custom chart of accounts?"). Rule: capitalise when it means ours, lowercase for the generic idea.

- Roles, lowercase: "account manager", "onboarding specialist", "quality control manager", "certified coders", "billing specialists".
- Tools, as the vendors spell them: "Bill.com", "Gusto". "client portal" lowercase.

### 4.8 Words the brand avoids

Observed: these are absent or near-absent across all 39 files, and should stay that way.

- Price-cutting words: "cheap" and "affordable" (1 use sitewide between them). The site says "cost-effective, flat monthly fee" and "clear, upfront estimate".
- Guarantees: "guarantee" appears nowhere. Do not promise outcomes.
- Hype: "world-class", "best-in-class", "cutting-edge", "game-changing", "revolutionary", "synergy" all appear nowhere.
- "customers" (3 uses sitewide, all slips). ODs have patients; ForEyes has clients.
- Slang names for the reader: "doc", "docs", "eye docs".
- Humour at the reader's expense, emoji, all-caps emphasis.
- Exclamation marks: only 7 in non-testimonial copy across the whole site, 3 of them in FAQ answers.

Recommended to retire (present on the current site, wrong for the new brand):

- Anything tax: "tax services", "tax planning", "tax strategy", "tax preparation", "tax experts", "our CPA", "personal returns". "tax" appears 476 times sitewide. The only tax word that survives is **"tax-ready"**, as a description of the books.
- "Solutions" as a noun for what we sell (579 uses sitewide, inflated by the old company name). Say the service.
- "accounting firm", "accounting and advisory firm", "accounting advisory, and solution partner". The new firm does books and billing.
- Filler adjectives and verbs (see section 7): "holistic", "robust", "comprehensive", "seamless", "actionable", "tailored", "unique", "leverage", "unlock", "optimize", "streamline", "ensure/ensuring", "empower".
- Competitor comparisons by city: "Nashville, TN bookkeepers".
- "number crunchers" (even as a denial, it puts the cliché in the reader's head).
- Catastrophe words: "nightmare", "drowning", "overwhelming".

## 5. Sentence-level habits

### 5.1 Sentences

- **Length.** Service pages average roughly 18 to 20 words per sentence; the A/R blog post averages about 14, with frequent sentences of eight words or fewer. Aim for the blog's rhythm on the new site: mostly 10 to 20 words, with a short sentence to land the point.
- **The short closer.** A paragraph often ends on a blunt line: "Know your numbers." / "Prevention beats collection every time." / "Speed matters only when the numbers are right." / "Most firms don't close the books until the end of the month."
- **Problem first, then what we do.** The standard paragraph shape on service pages is one sentence of the reader's problem, then "We…" or "Our…" with the mechanism, then the result. Example (Billing): "Every rejected claim impacts your cash flow. We meticulously review claims for accuracy before submission and post payments promptly, reducing denials and helping you get paid faster".
- **"so" clauses for the benefit.** "so your monthly P&L reads like a story you can act on", "so the balance in your books is the balance in your bank", "so you can rely on what you read". This is the brand's most reliable way to connect a feature to an outcome. Keep it, limit to one per paragraph.
- **Lists of three.** Everywhere: "support, systems, and financial clarity", "hire well, invest wisely, and plan an exit on your own terms", "Review, Ask, Decide". Vary it. A list of two or four reads as less formulaic.
- **Question then answer.** "Why? So you can focus on what matters most", "The result? More time for patients", "Sound familiar?", "Should you choose an S-Corp? Partnership? LLC?". Use at most once per page.
- **Contractions.** The older pages contract freely ("we're", "you'll", "don't"). The newer blocks sometimes do not ("Payments and paychecks do not wait on geography.", "do not work for free"). Rule: contract by default; use the full form only for deliberate emphasis.
- **Active voice, present tense.** "We manage your daily transactions, code them to a structure designed for eyecare, keep your general ledger current, and deliver reviewed reports on a fixed schedule."

### 5.2 Punctuation

- **Serial (Oxford) comma: yes**, consistently ("exam, optical, and contact lens activity"). One slip on About ("speed, flexibility and customized support").
- **Dashes are inconsistent** and need a single rule. Sitewide: 95 unspaced en dashes ("care for patients–not to spend late nights"), 51 unspaced em dashes ("easier to run—not harder to understand"), 40 spaced em dashes ("you're not alone — and you're not just looking"). The older core pages use the en dash; blogs and the INVISION page use em dashes. Recommendation: stop using the dash as the default connector. Prefer a full stop or a comma. Where a dash is truly needed, use one style across the whole site and no more than one per paragraph.
- **The "we don't just X–we Y" dash** is the most over-used construction on the site (30 uses in 16 files; see section 7). Do not write it.
- **Exclamation marks:** none in marketing copy. Remove the FAQ's "Yes!" and "Of course!".
- **Numbers:** numerals, with a plus sign for floors: "25+", "160+", "15+", "6 to 8 weeks", "10th business day", "45-minute", "90+ bucket", "5 Core Values". One inconsistency: "the first ten business days" beside "the 10th business day".
- **Ampersand** in headings and names only ("Billing & Collections", "Claims Submission & Payment Posting", "P&L"). "and" in sentences. The brand name always takes the ampersand.
- **Slash** in "A/R" only. "AP" has none.
- **Quotation marks** around a phrase being rejected: We don’t “also work with eyecare professionals.”
- **Apostrophes and quotes:** typographic (curly) on the main site, straight in places. Use typographic throughout.
- **Phone and hours:** "844-505-3937" with hyphens. The footer's "Hours: Mon-Fri at 8AM-5PM CT" should read as a plain range without "at".

## 8. One-page checklist: how to write a section for ForEyes Books & Billing

### Before writing

- [ ] Name the one reader: an independent OD who owns the practice, or their practice manager. Write to "you".
- [ ] Name the one thing this section must leave them knowing. If it is "we care" or "we are experts", pick something else.
- [ ] Find the eyecare-specific detail: exam versus optical versus contact lens revenue, vision plan payments, frames and lab bills, front-desk eligibility checks, aging buckets. If the section would read the same for a dental practice, it is not finished.
- [ ] Find the proof: a number, a day of the month, a named role, a named tool, a named client.

### Structure

- [ ] **Headline:** a concrete promise in Title Case, 4 to 9 words. Model: "Books That Show What Your Practice Actually Earns". Not a label ("Process Overview"), not a question ("Why Us?").
- [ ] **Opening sentence:** the owner's situation, in their terms. Model: "Exams, optical sales, and contact lenses all deposit into the same bank account, which makes it hard to see which part of your practice carries its weight."
- [ ] **Then what we do,** active voice, starting "We" or "Our", with the mechanism. Model: "Each month we match every account to its statement, so the balance in your books is the balance in your bank."
- [ ] **Then what the owner gets or does.** Model: "Your job is to review reports and ask questions."
- [ ] **Optional short closer.** Model: "Speed matters only when the numbers are right."
- [ ] 40 to 90 words for a standard section. Feature cards: a 2 to 6 word heading and one or two sentences.
- [ ] End the page, not every section, with the primary CTA.

### Sentence checks

- [ ] "We" for ForEyes, "you" for the reader, "patients" for the reader's customers, "clients" or "practices" for ours.
- [ ] Mostly 10 to 20 words per sentence. At least one short sentence per section.
- [ ] Contractions on. Oxford comma on. No exclamation marks.
- [ ] No "we don't just X, we Y". No rhetorical "The result?" more than once per page.
- [ ] At most one dash per paragraph, one dash style sitewide. Prefer a full stop.
- [ ] Every technical term is either the owner's own vocabulary or defined on first use: "revenue cycle management (RCM)", "accounts receivable (A/R)".
- [ ] Search the draft for: holistic, robust, comprehensive, seamless, actionable, tailored, unique, leverage, unlock, optimize, streamline, ensure, empower, solutions, peace of mind, headaches, clarity (max one), clear picture (max one). Replace each with the specific thing.
- [ ] No superlatives ("leading", "best", "go-to"), no guarantees, no comparisons with named or located competitors.
- [ ] No city keywords in prose.

### Naming checks

- [ ] "ForEyes Books & Billing" on first mention, then "ForEyes" or "we". Never "For Eyes", never "ForEyes Solutions". Same spelling in the page title and meta description.
- [ ] "Wade Weisz, OD", then "Wade". Clients as "Name, OD".
- [ ] "eyecare" one word. "multi-location" hyphenated. "Discovery Call" capitalised. "Custom Chart of Accounts" capitalised when it is ours. "P&L". "A/R". "AP". "Bill.com". "Gusto".
- [ ] Services by their exact names: Bookkeeping, Insurance Billing, Accounts Payable, Payroll, Practice Consulting.

### Wordplay check

- [ ] At most one vision pun on the page, in a headline or sign-off, and it is literally true.
- [ ] None in CTAs, prices, timelines, process steps, or the tax statement.

### CTA check

- [ ] Primary button is exactly "Book a Discovery Call". Secondary is the pricing calculator.
- [ ] Service links name the outcome in the owner's words ("Get Your Books in Order", "Stop Chasing Payments").
- [ ] A reassurance sits near the primary CTA (free, no obligation, how long it takes), using confirmed facts.

### Positioning the two flagships
